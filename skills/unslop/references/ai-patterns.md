# AI-pattern catalog

Use this catalog to diagnose a draft. Patterns are evidence only in context.
Do not replace every listed word, flatten an established voice, or change
quoted language.

## Content patterns

1. **Puffery:** claims such as "pivotal moment," "testament to," "setting the
   stage," or "indelible mark" that announce importance instead of stating an
   event or consequence.
2. **Promotional description:** "vibrant," "breathtaking," "groundbreaking,"
   "renowned," "stunning," and similar praise without evidence.
3. **Name-dropping:** lists of publications, companies, or authorities without
   explaining what any source established.
4. **Vague attribution:** "experts believe," "reports suggest," or "critics
   argue" without a named source. Name it, qualify it, or remove the claim.
5. **Superficial participles:** trailing "highlighting," "showcasing,"
   "reflecting," "ensuring," or "fostering" clauses that imply a consequence
   without establishing it.
6. **Formulaic resilience:** "despite challenges, X continues to thrive" and
   similar conflict-free conclusions. State the actual problem and outcome.
7. **Generic conclusion:** "the future looks bright," "an exciting step
   forward," or a closing paragraph that repeats the introduction.
8. **Feeling instead of mechanism:** phrases such as "stays close at hand" or
   "types that follow your schema" where an operation, invariant, or number
   would tell the reader what happens.

## Language patterns

9. **Stock AI vocabulary:** additionally, crucial, delve, enduring, enhance,
   fostering, garner, interplay, intricate, landscape, pivotal, showcase,
   tapestry, testament, underscore, and vibrant. Replace them when they are
   vague or ornamental, not when the domain genuinely requires the word.
10. **Inflated copulas:** "serves as," "stands as," "boasts," or "features"
    where "is" or "has" states the same fact.
11. **Contrast template:** repeated "not just X, but Y" constructions that
    manufacture drama. State the stronger point directly.
12. **Forced groups:** unrelated ideas arranged into threes or another tidy
    number for cadence rather than meaning.
13. **Synonym cycling:** renaming the same concept across a paragraph to avoid
    repetition. Pick the clearest term and reuse it.
14. **False range:** "from X to Y" when the endpoints do not form a scale or
    meaningful span.
15. **Filler:** "in order to," "due to the fact that," "it is important to
    note," and other phrases removable without changing meaning.
16. **Stacked hedges:** "could potentially possibly" or several qualifications
    performing one uncertainty judgment. Keep the calibrated hedge that fits.
17. **Weak verb plus adverb:** "significantly improves" without a measured
    delta or "runs quickly" where a stronger verb or number is available.
18. **Needlessly formal synonym:** utilize, leverage, facilitate, numerous, or
    "in the event that" where use, help, many, or if is clearer.

## Structural and visual patterns

19. **Dash dependence:** em dashes repeatedly carrying qualifications or
    transitions that deserve sentences or commas. Do not ban a deliberate dash
    in an established voice.
20. **Colon dependence:** colons used as dramatic mid-sentence connectors.
    Keep them for real lists, definitions, examples, and established notation.
21. **Parenthetical escape:** replacing every dash with parentheses instead of
    clarifying the sentence.
22. **Inline-header list:** repeated bold labels followed by a colon and a
    sentence that restates the label. Keep compact definition lists when the
    labels genuinely aid scanning.
23. **Formatting theater:** excessive boldface, title-case headings, decorative
    emoji, or ornamental separators that add hierarchy without meaning.
24. **Canned architecture:** every answer forced into overview, benefits,
    challenges, future, and conclusion regardless of the material.

## Communication artifacts

25. **Chatbot framing:** "Of course," "Certainly," "I hope this helps," "Let
    me know if," or theatrical discovery claims such as "the smoking gun."
26. **Sycophancy:** "Great question," "You're absolutely right," or praise used
    before answering rather than supported agreement or disagreement.
27. **Cutoff disclaimer:** "while details are limited" followed by speculation.
    Obtain the evidence, bound the statement, or omit it.
28. **Fake quotation:** invented phrases in quotation marks to imitate a user,
    critic, or stakeholder.

## Technical abstraction patterns

29. **Metaphor as explanation:** substrate, wedge, vector, locus, nexus,
    bedrock, scaffolding, modality, paradigm, north star, flywheel, endgame,
    ratchet, or "gold-plating" used instead of naming the component or action.
30. **Generic technical nouns:** primitive, surface, harness, layer, pipeline,
    and architecture used without identifying which interface, test command,
    boundary, or module is meant. These words are legitimate when defined by
    the code or domain.
31. **Passive actor deletion:** passive voice that hides who performs an action
    or enforces a rule. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown,
    irrelevant, or intentionally de-emphasized.
32. **Dense clause chain:** a sentence with several conditions and conclusions
    that makes the reader backtrack. Split by idea or remove subordinate detail.
33. **Portable non-information:** a sentence that could appear unchanged in an
    unrelated project's documentation. Replace it with a project-specific
    mechanism or remove it.

## Replacement test

For every proposed edit, ask:

- What concrete information becomes clearer?
- Is the pattern actually present, or did one flagged word trigger the edit?
- Does the replacement preserve the writer's meaning and confidence?
- Would a domain expert consider the original term more precise?

If the edit cannot answer those questions, leave the text alone.
