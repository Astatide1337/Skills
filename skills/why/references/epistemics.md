# Epistemics

## Confidence tiers

Classify conclusions by the strongest evidence supporting them:

- **Direct:** a source states the reason. Quote the relevant words and cite the
  source beside the claim.
- **Supported:** independent evidence converges on the same explanation. Show
  the chain and cite each material link.
- **Inferred:** the explanation fits the evidence but was not stated. Name the
  observations and the assumption connecting them.
- **Speculative:** several stories remain plausible. Present them as
  alternatives, not a ranked fact unless evidence supports the ranking.
- **Unknown:** the search did not establish a reason. Say what was searched,
  which sources were unavailable, and what evidence could resolve it.

Use causal words such as "because" only for direct or strongly supported
claims. For inference, use calibrated language such as "appears," "likely," or
"suggests," followed by the evidence. Confidence should track source quality,
independence, proximity to the decision, and contradiction, not the fluency of
the story.

## Contradictions and absence

Do not hide disagreement. Put contradictory sources next to each other, note
their dates and vantage points, then explain whether later evidence supersedes
earlier evidence or the conflict remains unresolved.

Absence of a rationale is not proof that no rationale existed. A null search is
useful only when the searched scope is named. Distinguish "not found in the
available history" from "never documented."

## Common rationalization traps

- Inferring intent from current behavior.
- Assuming a clean architecture was deliberately designed that way.
- Treating a commit message as a complete account of the decision.
- Treating the change that introduced code as proof of why its exact shape was
  chosen.
- Selecting only evidence that agrees with the user's proposed explanation.
- Turning a workaround into a timeless requirement.
- Crediting one person or event when the change accumulated over time.

Do not agree with a proposed rationale merely because it is plausible. Test it
against alternatives and report disconfirming evidence with equal prominence.

## Calibration checklist

Before reporting:

1. Can every direct or supported claim be traced to an adjacent citation?
2. Did the investigation search at least source history and any supplied
   decision or incident evidence?
3. Are facts, interpretations, and unknowns visibly distinct?
4. Did contradictory or missing evidence reduce confidence?
5. Does the explanation distinguish the historical reason from the current
   constraint?
6. Would a reader know what new evidence could change the conclusion?
